# Perceived Distance as Retained Calibration

## A Verrell's Law Concept Note

**Author:** Marcos Verrell Moss Ross (M.R.)  
**Maintained by:** Inappropriate Media Limited / Collapse Aware AI  
**Date:** 13 July 2026  
**Status:** Conceptual research note — not empirical validation

## Summary

Distance is normally spoken about as though it is directly experienced. In practice, a person does not receive distance itself as a raw sensory signal. The nervous system receives partial cues—visual perspective, relative size, motion, sound direction, loudness, reverberation, echo, bodily movement and touch—and uses retained calibration to construct a usable estimate of spatial separation.

This makes perceived distance a useful example of the retained-state selection principle described by Verrell's Law:

> Present sensory information does not operate alone. Retained experience helps weight the available cues and influences the spatial interpretation selected for action.

The physical environment may remain unchanged while different observers form materially different usable representations of it. This can occur because their available sensory channels, retained histories and learned calibration differ.

## Origin of the Note

This note arose from observing a blind participant describing the difficulty of experiencing distance across a large open landscape. The participant could detect sounds and could notice directional changes when turning, but described being unable to experience the distance of the roads, vehicles, buildings and wider landscape in the way a sighted observer might.

The statement should not be generalised into a claim that blind people cannot perceive distance. Blind and visually impaired people can form spatial estimates through hearing, movement, touch, cane feedback, environmental echoes, learned routes and, in some cases, deliberate echolocation. The relevant point is narrower:

> Removing or reducing one major observation channel changes the evidence available for constructing spatial depth.

The distinction between congenital blindness and later sight loss also matters. A person blind from birth may develop spatial calibration without stored visual experience. A person who loses sight later may retain visual concepts and memories of distance while no longer receiving the continuous visual cues that previously supported them. These are different retained-state conditions and should not be treated as equivalent.

## Distance Is Inferred from Cues

Visual distance can be informed by:

- relative size;
- perspective;
- occlusion;
- stereoscopic disparity;
- texture gradients;
- atmospheric clarity;
- familiar object size;
- motion parallax and optic flow.

Auditory distance can be informed by:

- changing sound intensity;
- the balance between direct and reverberant sound;
- echoes and reflection patterns;
- spectral changes introduced by the environment;
- the listener's own movement;
- prior knowledge of the likely source volume.

These cues are not equally reliable. A quiet nearby vehicle and a loud distant vehicle can produce similar sound levels. An open landscape may provide fewer useful reflections than an enclosed room. Turning the head can improve directional information without providing a precise absolute distance. The system must therefore combine uncertain information rather than read distance from a single measurement.

## Retained State and Spatial Selection

Under Verrell's Law, the spatial estimate can be represented as a selected outcome among competing interpretations:

\[
z_i = U(y_i; S_t, O_t) + \lambda B(y_i; M_t)
\]

\[
P(y_i \mid S_t, O_t, M_t)
=
\frac{\exp(z_i)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j)}
\]

For this application:

- \(y_i\) is a candidate spatial interpretation, such as near, intermediate or far;
- \(S_t\) is the present bodily and environmental state held relevant by the test design;
- \(O_t\) is the current sensory information;
- \(M_t\) is retained sensory calibration and prior spatial experience;
- \(B(y_i; M_t)\) is the influence of that retained calibration on a candidate interpretation;
- \(\lambda\) represents the strength of retained-state influence in the tested task.

The model does not claim that people consciously calculate these terms. It provides a formal way to describe the hypothesis that present cues are interpreted through retained state rather than operating as history-free inputs.

## The Role of Time and Repeated Frames

Distance should not be reduced to time alone. A stationary observer can obtain some depth information from a single visual scene. However, many strong distance cues depend on sequential change:

- objects shift relative to one another as the observer moves;
- sound intensity and direction change across successive positions;
- echoes change as the observer approaches a surface;
- travel time and bodily effort accumulate across a route;
- repeated encounters calibrate what particular cue patterns normally mean.

The careful claim is therefore:

> The experience of distance is often strengthened by information distributed across time, movement and repeated observation.

Successive frames do not create physical distance, but they can provide the changing information from which an observer constructs a more reliable estimate of it.

## Why This Matters to Verrell's Law

This example helps separate an external property from an observer's usable representation of that property.

The environment may contain the same road, wall, vehicle and distant building for every observer. What differs is the available input and the retained state used to interpret it. The resulting selection chain is:

> Sensory interaction → retained calibration → cue weighting → spatial estimate → action

A sighted person, a person blind from birth and a person with later sight loss may therefore produce different spatial estimates under the same external conditions without requiring the external landscape itself to change.

This is compatible with the central Verrell's Law claim:

> Prior interactions can leave retained state changes, and those retained changes can bias future selection.

Here, the selected outcome is not a change in physical reality. It is the observer's action-relevant interpretation of spatial distance.

## Falsifiable Research Direction

The concept could be tested without making claims about universal field memory or quantum collapse.

A suitable protocol could compare participants with different sensory histories while presenting matched present-day spatial cues. Candidate groups might include sighted participants, congenitally blind participants and participants with later sight loss.

Possible measurements include:

- estimated distance to controlled sound sources;
- confidence in each estimate;
- changes produced by head movement or walking;
- performance in open and reverberant environments;
- improvement across repeated trials;
- retention of calibration after a delay;
- transfer of learned calibration to unfamiliar environments.

The relevant prediction would be that different retained sensory histories produce repeatable differences in cue weighting, estimation pattern or adaptation under matched present input.

A null result would also be meaningful. If matched groups showed no repeatable history-dependent divergence beyond the pre-registered null, retained-state influence would not be supported in that tested regime.

## Boundary of the Claim

This note does **not** claim that:

- blind people have no spatial perception;
- distance is created by observation;
- physical distance is only a form of time;
- perception directly changes the external landscape;
- one personal account validates Verrell's Law;
- the example establishes an electromagnetic, field-based or quantum mechanism.

It identifies a disciplined conceptual application: perceived distance is assembled from incomplete present information, and retained calibration can influence which spatial interpretation becomes usable for action.

## Compact Form

> Distance exists externally, but experienced distance is constructed internally. Present sensory cues provide partial information; retained experience weights those cues; the resulting selection becomes the observer's usable spatial frame.
