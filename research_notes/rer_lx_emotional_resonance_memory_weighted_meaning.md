# Research Note: Reduced Emotional Resonance, Memory-Weighted Meaning, and Verrell’s Law

**Reference paper:**  
Wilhelmiina Toivo, Christoph Scheepers & Jean-Marc Dewaele,  
*RER-LX: A new scale to measure reduced emotional resonance in bilinguals’ later learnt language*

---

## Summary

This research note records a relevant external paper for the Verrell’s Law / Collapse Aware AI public proof pack.

The paper develops and validates **RER-LX**, a psychometric scale for measuring **Reduced Emotional Resonance in LX** — where **LX** refers to a later-learned language and **L1** refers to a first language.

The central finding is that bilingual and multilingual speakers may experience emotional words, phrases, criticism, swearing, affection, poetry, films, and interpersonal connection as emotionally stronger in their first language than in a later-learned language.

This is relevant to Verrell’s Law because it supports a key compatible principle:

> Meaning is not flat. Emotional force is shaped by memory, embodiment, acquisition history, lived context, and prior exposure.

This paper does **not** prove Verrell’s Law.  
It does **not** mention Verrell’s Law.  
It does **not** prove field memory.  
It does **not** describe Collapse Aware AI.

However, it does provide useful independent support for the idea that present meaning is affected by accumulated past information.

## Why this paper matters

The paper argues that emotional resonance differs between a first language and a later-learned language because the first language is usually acquired in a richer emotional and sensory environment.

First-language emotional words are often connected to:

- early social experience
- family interaction
- sensory context
- bodily response
- autobiographical memory
- affective conditioning
- implicit memory
- emotional socialisation

Later-learned language, especially when learned through formal instruction, may be more detached, more declarative, and less deeply connected to embodied emotional experience.

This is important because it shows that the same semantic content can carry different emotional force depending on memory history.

In Verrell’s Law terms:

> The present signal is not neutral. It is biased by weighted past conditions.

## Core relevance to Verrell’s Law

Verrell’s Law proposes that memory, information, and prior weighted conditions influence future collapse.

The RER-LX paper gives a grounded psychological example of this principle in human language:

> The same phrase can be understood intellectually while carrying different emotional weight depending on the memory structure behind it.

For example, the phrase “I love you” may be understood in both a first language and a later-learned language, but the emotional intensity can differ.

Likewise, swear words, criticism, insults, romantic songs, poetry, and emotional conversation may carry different force across languages.

This supports the broader idea that meaning is not only semantic.

Meaning is also:

- memory-weighted
- emotionally conditioned
- context-sensitive
- embodied
- socially reinforced
- historically shaped

That is directly compatible with the public-facing Verrell’s Law claim that past information is not neutral.

## Relevant points from the paper

The paper is useful for the proof pack because it highlights several concepts that align with the Verrell’s Law / Collapse Aware AI direction:

1. **Emotional words are memory-linked**
   - First-language emotional words are described as being connected to autobiographical memory, sensory modalities, and affect.

2. **Emotional force can vary across contexts**
   - The same emotional concept can feel stronger or weaker depending on the language in which it is used.

3. **Later-learned language may feel more detached**
   - LX words may be processed more intellectually or declaratively, with weaker emotional embodiment.

4. **Emotional resonance is measurable**
   - The paper develops a 15-item scale to quantify reduced emotional resonance in LX.

5. **Meaning is not purely lexical**
   - Emotional meaning depends on more than dictionary definition or literal translation.
