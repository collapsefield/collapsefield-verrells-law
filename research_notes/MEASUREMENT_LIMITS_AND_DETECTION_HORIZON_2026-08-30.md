# Measurement Limits and the Detection Horizon — Research Note

**Status:** Exploratory methodology note; non-canonical  
**Date:** 30 August 2026  
**Author:** Marcos Verrell Moss Ross (M.R.) / Inappropriate Media Limited

---

## Purpose

A recurring question around broad retained-state hypotheses is whether an effect could exist while current instruments, sensors or experimental designs remain too insensitive to detect it.

That possibility is scientifically legitimate in principle. It is also easy to misuse.

This note sets a strict boundary:

> **Instrument sensitivity may limit what can be observed, but “the detector is not good enough yet” must never be used as an unfalsifiable escape from a null result.**

Detector insufficiency is one hypothesis among several. Another is that the proposed effect is absent, smaller than predicted, incorrectly specified, or already explained by known variables.

---

## The Detection-Horizon Problem

Every empirical programme has a practical detection horizon determined by factors such as:

- sensor sensitivity;
- calibration error;
- sampling rate;
- spatial resolution;
- temporal resolution;
- signal-to-noise ratio;
- uncontrolled environmental variables;
- biological variability;
- measurement back-action;
- proxy validity;
- statistical power;
- minimum detectable effect;
- multiple-comparison burden.

A predicted effect can be physically real yet remain below the current detection threshold.

But the reverse inference is invalid:

> **Failure to detect an effect does not establish that an undetected effect exists.**

---

## Why the Gravitational-Wave Analogy Must Be Used Carefully

Gravitational waves are a useful historical example of measurement sensitivity catching up with a theory, but the analogy should not be overstated.

General relativity supplied a mature quantitative theory with specific predicted signatures. Decades of instrument development, calibration, noise rejection and cross-detector analysis were required before direct detection became possible.

The lesson relevant to retained-state research is methodological:

1. define the predicted observable before building the interpretation around a result;
2. specify the expected scale or at least an upper/lower range where possible;
3. design sensors and controls around that signature;
4. estimate whether the apparatus can detect the predicted effect;
5. report nulls and upper bounds honestly;
6. replicate independently.

The lesson is **not** that every currently undetected mechanism will eventually be discovered.

## Biological / Cognitive Retained-State Research

If retained informational history is hypothesised to affect biological or cognitive selection, candidate observables might include conventional measurable variables such as:

- neural activity;
- autonomic signals;
- endocrine/biochemical state;
- behavioural choice distributions;
- reaction time;
- electrophysiology;
- network connectivity measures;
- physiological synchrony;
- environmental state.

These are examples of measurable channels, not evidence that any unknown “biosignal memory field” exists.

A new or unknown channel should only be proposed after known variables are controlled as far as practicable and a reproducible residual effect remains.

---

## A Safe Experimental Sequence

### 1. Define the selection outcome

Specify what later event counts as a candidate-relative selection.

### 2. Define retained history operationally

State exactly what differs between the history conditions.

### 3. Measure conventional variables first

Record known physiological, environmental and behavioural variables that could explain the effect.

### 4. Estimate minimum detectable effect

Before confirmatory testing, calculate the smallest effect the instrumentation and sample can reliably detect.

### 5. Pre-register the predicted direction

Avoid interpreting arbitrary post-hoc correlations as the proposed mechanism.

### 6. Use ablation/intervention where possible

Deliberately alter, remove or randomise the retained-state condition.

### 7. Replicate with independent apparatus or laboratories

An instrument-specific artifact should not survive independent replication.

### 8. Publish upper bounds after nulls

If no effect is detected, report what size of effect the experiment was capable of ruling out.

## Unknown Mechanism vs Unknown Measurement

Three cases must remain distinct:

### Case A — Known mechanism, insufficient measurement

The underlying mechanism is established independently but the local apparatus lacks sensitivity.

### Case B — Proposed mechanism, measurable residual

A reproducible effect remains after known confounds are controlled, but its mechanism is not identified.

This supports further investigation, not a specific field interpretation by itself.

### Case C — Proposed mechanism, no detectable residual

No effect remains above the detection threshold.

The correct conclusion is that the hypothesis is not supported at that sensitivity in that regime.

## Relevance to Verrell’s Law

The canonical Verrell’s Law framework does not require an unknown detector or an unknown field.

Its first empirical task is simpler:

> **Under matched present conditions, does a preregistered retained-state difference predict a directional change in later selection?**

That question can be tested wherever retained state and selection are already measurable.

Only after a reproducible effect is established independently does it become useful to ask whether the known variables fully explain it or whether a new measurement channel is required.

This ordering protects the research programme from becoming mechanism-first speculation.

## Relevance to CAAI

Collapse Aware AI does not require any unknown physical detector.

CAAI is deliberately engineered so retained-state influence, ablation/reference conditions, selection outcomes and evidence can be inspected directly in software.

That makes it an engineering test environment for retained-state selection, not evidence of an unknown biological or physical signal.

---

## Research Rule

> **A future detector may reveal something current instruments miss. That possibility is a reason to improve measurement, not a reason to treat an unmeasured mechanism as already established.**
