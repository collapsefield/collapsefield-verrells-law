# Established Cognitive Precedents Relevant to Retained-State Selection

**Date:** 13 August 2026  
**Status:** Research context / external precedent — **not validation of Verrell’s Law**

## Purpose

Verrell’s Law proposes that:

> **Under otherwise equivalent present conditions, retained informational history is hypothesised to exert a measurable, directional, intervention-sensitive bias on future state selection.**

Human cognitive-memory research does **not** establish Verrell’s Law.

However, several well-established findings demonstrate a closely related empirical principle: prior informational state can systematically alter later recall, judgement, accessibility and reconstruction.

These findings are therefore treated as **external scientific precedents and analogues**, not as validation of the Law.

---

## 1. Hindsight-dependent reconstruction

### Fischhoff & Beyth — remembered probabilities

Participants originally estimated probabilities for possible outcomes of President Nixon’s trips to Peking and Moscow.

After the trips had occurred, they were unexpectedly asked to reconstruct their original estimates.

Their remembered probabilities shifted systematically:

- events believed to have occurred were remembered as having been assigned higher probabilities;
- events believed not to have occurred tended to be reconstructed with lower probabilities.

### Relevance

The original event did not simply remain a fixed record.

Later retained knowledge about the outcome influenced reconstruction of the earlier informational state in a directional manner.

This provides an established example of:

**earlier state + subsequently retained outcome information → altered later cognitive state**

It is particularly relevant to Verrell’s Law because the influence was structured rather than random.

**Reference:** Fischhoff, B. & Beyth, R. (1975). *I knew it would happen: Remembered probabilities of once-future things.* Organizational Behavior and Human Performance, 13(1), 1–16. DOI: 10.1016/0030-5073(75)90002-1.

## 2. Post-event information can alter later memory

### Loftus & Palmer — automobile reconstruction

Participants watched films of automobile accidents.

The subsequent question varied only in wording, using verbs such as:

- contacted
- hit
- bumped
- collided
- smashed

The wording systematically changed participants’ estimated vehicle speeds.

In the second experiment, participants exposed to the stronger wording were also more likely one week later to report seeing broken glass even though no broken glass appeared in the film.

### Relevance

This is stronger than simple correlation.

The experiment deliberately altered post-event informational input and measured the resulting change in later memory.

The sequence can be represented as:

**same observed event → controlled informational intervention → different later remembered state**

That is directly relevant to the intervention-sensitive component of the Verrell’s Law testing framework.

**Reference:** Loftus, E. F. & Palmer, J. C. (1974). *Reconstruction of automobile destruction: An example of the interaction between language and memory.* Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 13(5), 585–589. DOI: 10.1016/S0022-5371(74)80011-3.

## 3. False presuppositions can become incorporated into later reports

### Loftus — leading questions

In subsequent experiments involving hundreds of participants, wording introduced true or false presuppositions about previously viewed events.

False presuppositions increased the probability that participants later reported having seen the suggested object.

### Relevance

Information introduced **after** an event can alter the later representation of that event.

This demonstrates that retained informational state is not necessarily passive archival storage.

Later information can interact with previously encoded information and alter subsequent output.

**Reference:** Loftus, E. F. (1975). *Leading questions and the eyewitness report.* Cognitive Psychology, 7(4), 560–572. DOI: 10.1016/0010-0285(75)90023-7.

## 4. Previous choices directionally bias later memory

### Choice-supportive memory

Research on choice-supportive memory has found systematic distortions when people remember previous decisions.

Participants are more likely to attribute positive features to options they previously chose and negative features to alternatives they rejected.

Critically, this is not simply general positivity: experiments comparing chosen and assigned options have found different memory patterns.

### Relevance

A prior decision becomes part of retained history and subsequently influences reconstruction of information associated with that decision.

The direction of the later bias is related to the earlier choice.

Conceptually:

**prior choice state → retained choice history → directionally biased later reconstruction**

This provides another established example in which retained history is associated with structured effects on later cognitive output.

**Reference:** Mather, M., Shafir, E. & Johnson, M. K. (2003). *Remembering chosen and assigned options.* Memory & Cognition, 31(3), 422–433. DOI: 10.3758/BF03194400.

---

## 5. Semantic history alters subsequent accessibility

### Collins & Loftus — spreading activation

The spreading-activation model of semantic processing proposes that activating one concept in semantic memory spreads activation to related concepts.

Related representations can consequently become more accessible for subsequent processing.

### Relevance

This offers a useful mechanistic precedent for one aspect of retained-state systems:

**activation/history at one point changes the accessibility landscape for subsequent processing.**

This is conceptually relevant to semantic association and retained-state eligibility.

It should **not**, however, be cited as direct evidence that semantic activation creates false memories or as experimental validation of Verrell’s Law.

**Reference:** Collins, A. M. & Loftus, E. F. (1975). *A spreading-activation theory of semantic processing.* Psychological Review, 82(6), 407–428. DOI: 10.1037/0033-295X.82.6.407.

# Relation to Verrell’s Law

Taken together, these findings establish several relevant empirical precedents:

1. Information retained from previous events can influence later cognitive outputs.
2. The resulting influence can be directional rather than random.
3. New information can alter the subsequent effect of earlier information.
4. Controlled manipulation of informational history can produce measurable changes in later reports or judgements.
5. Semantic association can alter which related information becomes accessible.
6. Previous selections themselves can become retained history that biases later reconstruction.

These observations are compatible with the central intuition behind Verrell’s Law.

They do **not**, by themselves, demonstrate the proposed Law.

The stronger Verrell’s Law claim requires controlled testing in which otherwise-equivalent present conditions are compared while retained informational history is independently manipulated.

The required evidence therefore remains:

**Divergence → Coherence → Prospective Prediction → Intervention/Ablation → broader replication**

The relevant scientific point is narrower but important:

> **History-dependent informational bias is already an experimentally established phenomenon in human cognition. Verrell’s Law asks whether a more general, measurable and intervention-sensitive retained-state selection principle can be formally isolated and replicated.**

---

## Claim boundary

This research note must not be represented as evidence that:

- Verrell’s Law has been experimentally validated;
- human memory operates according to CAAI architecture;
- CAAI proves a universal physical or cognitive law;
- semantic priming and retained-state selection are identical mechanisms;
- cognitive-memory bias establishes the proposed law outside the specific experimental domains in which it was observed.

The cited literature is **supporting scientific context and precedent**.

Formal validation of Verrell’s Law remains a separate empirical question.
