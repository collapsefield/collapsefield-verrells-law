# Retained-State Selection Across Cognitive Science and Machine Learning

## A cross-field research note for Verrell's Law

**M.R. — Verrell's Law Research Archive**  
**September 2026**

---

## 1. Why this note exists

A recurring idea appears across several areas of contemporary research:

> The present is not always sufficient to explain the next selection. Information retained from previous states, choices, stimuli or interactions can systematically alter what happens next.

Different disciplines describe this using different terminology.

Researchers study **choice-history bias**, **serial dependence**, **prior probability**, **continuity fields**, **primacy bias**, **memory effects**, and other forms of history-sensitive behaviour.

These mechanisms should not be treated as identical.

But they share a useful structural question:

**How much of a system's present behaviour is determined by its present input, and how much depends upon information retained from its previous trajectory?**

That question sits directly beside the central experimental question posed by Verrell's Law.

---

## 2. Verrell's Law

Verrell's Law investigates the hypothesis that:

> **Under otherwise equivalent present conditions, retained informational history can exert a measurable, directional and intervention-sensitive bias on subsequent state selection.**

The important phrase is **otherwise equivalent present conditions**.

If two systems receive equivalent present input but possess different retained histories, the framework asks whether those histories produce a measurable difference in subsequent selection.

The hypothesis is intended to be testable.

Evidence that history matters is not, by itself, sufficient.

A serious test must distinguish retained-state influence from ordinary differences in current conditions, uncontrolled variables, noise and post-hoc interpretation.

The broader evidence programme therefore progresses through:

1. **Divergence** — does retained history produce a measurable selection difference?
2. **Coherence** — is the difference structured and reproducible?
3. **Continuity prediction** — does retained trajectory improve prediction beyond present-state information alone?
4. **Intervention / ablation** — does manipulating retained history alter subsequent selection in the predicted direction?
5. **Symbolic salience** — where relevant, do differently weighted historical states produce correspondingly different effects?

This note does **not** claim that the research below proves Verrell's Law.

Instead, it documents independent areas of research in which history-dependent influence on subsequent processing or selection has already been observed.

---

# 3. Choice history and subsequent decision formation

## Urai et al. (2019) — Choice history biases subsequent evidence accumulation

Anne Urai and colleagues investigated how previous perceptual choices influence later decisions.

Their central finding is particularly relevant to retained-state selection.

The researchers found that perceptual decisions were not determined solely by the sensory evidence available during the current trial.

Previous choices systematically affected subsequent decision formation.

Importantly, their modelling indicated that choice history altered the **accumulation of evidence during the subsequent decision**, rather than merely changing the initial starting point of the decision process.

The effects also extended across choices several trials into the past.

This provides a clear empirical example of a general structure:

**previous state / choice → retained influence → altered subsequent processing → altered selection probability**

That is not proof of Verrell's Law as a general law.

It is, however, strong independent evidence that history-sensitive selection exists within experimentally controlled human decision systems.

**Reference**

Urai, A.E., de Gee, J.W., Tsetsos, K. & Donner, T.H. (2019).  
*Choice history biases subsequent evidence accumulation.*  
eLife 8:e46331.

https://elifesciences.org/articles/46331

# 4. Prior information and decision bias

## Mulder et al. (2012) — Bias in the brain

Mulder and colleagues examined how prior probability and potential reward alter perceptual decisions.

Using diffusion modelling and neuroimaging, the study investigated how advance information changes the decision process.

The work demonstrates a broader principle important to this research programme:

**information available before a present decision can alter the subsequent decision dynamics.**

This study concerns experimentally supplied prior information rather than retained history in the specific Verrell formulation, so the distinction matters.

Its relevance is therefore mechanistic and comparative rather than direct evidence for the Law.

**Reference**

Mulder, M.J., Wagenmakers, E.-J., Ratcliff, R., Boekel, W. & Forstmann, B.U. (2012).  
*Bias in the brain: a diffusion model analysis of prior probability and potential payoff.*  
Journal of Neuroscience, 32(7), 2335–2343.

https://www.jneurosci.org/content/32/7/2335

---

# 5. A 2026 result: previous choice and stimulus probability follow different paths

## Forster & Hellmann (2026) — Two Paths to Bias

Recent work by Carina Forster and Sebastian Hellmann provides another useful comparison.

Their study investigates two distinct historical/contextual influences:

- prior stimulus probability;
- previous choice.

Using drift-diffusion modelling, the researchers examine how these sources of bias affect later decision processes.

The significance for retained-state research is not that this paper validates Verrell's Law.

Rather, it demonstrates that **previous-choice history remains an active, experimentally measurable variable in current computational research on decision formation**, and that different sources of prior information need not influence selection through the same mechanism.

That distinction is important.

A retained-state theory should not assume that all historical information produces one universal effect.

Different histories may differ in:

- direction;
- magnitude;
- persistence;
- relevance;
- interaction with present evidence.

**Reference**

Forster, C. & Hellmann, S. (2026).  
*Two Paths to Bias: Distinct Influences of Stimulus Probability and Previous Choice on Drift Diffusion Parameters.*  
Computational Brain & Behavior.

https://doi.org/10.1007/s42113-026-00331-6

# 6. Serial dependence: perception carries its recent past

Another substantial research literature concerns **serial dependence**.

Current perception can be systematically biased by information encountered previously.

This is important because it provides an experimentally accessible example in which a system's current response cannot always be characterised purely from the current stimulus.

## Manassi & Whitney (2024) — Continuity fields

Manassi and Whitney review positive serial dependence across perception, action, decisions and memory.

They describe situations in which current representations are systematically biased toward recently encountered information.

Their continuity-field account proposes that integration across time can stabilize perception against noisy and continuously changing sensory input.

For the purposes of Verrell's Law, the important observation is structural:

**recent informational history can remain behaviourally relevant to present processing.**

Again, the mechanisms proposed within continuity-field research should not be conflated with Verrell's Law.

The overlap is the experimentally observed importance of retained temporal context.

**Reference**

Manassi, M. & Whitney, D. (2024).  
*Continuity fields enhance visual perception through positive serial dependence.*  
Nature Reviews Psychology, 3, 352–366.

https://www.nature.com/articles/s44159-024-00297-x

---

# 7. History does not always attract — it can repel

## Ceylan & Pascucci (2023)

An especially important qualification comes from research into attractive and repulsive serial dependence.

Ceylan and Pascucci found that the influence of previous stimuli depends on factors including:

- behavioural relevance;
- elapsed time;
- the number of intervening stimuli.

Previous information can produce **attractive or repulsive** effects.

This matters for any serious retained-state framework.

If history influences future selection, the prediction should not simply be:

> "the system repeats its past."

Historical influence may instead:

- attract;
- repel;
- decay;
- interact;
- become suppressed;
- be strengthened or weakened by intervening events.

The direction of influence therefore becomes an empirical question.

This is compatible with treating retained history as a **weighted influence on selection**, rather than as a deterministic command.

**Reference**

Ceylan, G. & Pascucci, D. (2023).  
*Attractive and repulsive serial dependence: the role of task relevance, the passage of time, and the number of stimuli.*  
Journal of Vision, 23(6):8.

https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10278548/

# 8. Machine learning: early history can disproportionately matter

History dependence is not restricted to biological cognition.

## Nikishin et al. (2022) — Primacy bias in deep reinforcement learning

Nikishin and colleagues identified what they call **primacy bias** in deep reinforcement learning.

Their experiments showed that deep reinforcement-learning agents can become disproportionately influenced by early interactions and subsequently fail to make full use of useful evidence encountered later.

This provides a computational example of trajectory dependence:

**two systems exposed to different early histories can develop different later behaviour even when later evidence becomes available.**

The mechanism is specific to reinforcement-learning architectures and training dynamics.

It should therefore not be presented as evidence of a universal physical law.

Nevertheless, it demonstrates independently that **historical weighting can materially alter future computational behaviour**.

**Reference**

Nikishin, E., Schwarzer, M., D'Oro, P., Bacon, P.-L. & Courville, A. (2022).  
*The Primacy Bias in Deep Reinforcement Learning.*  
Proceedings of the 39th International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), PMLR 162.

https://proceedings.mlr.press/v162/nikishin22a.html

# 9. Large language models and sequence effects

More recent research has begun investigating related effects in large language models.

These results should presently be treated more cautiously because some of the relevant work exists as preprints rather than established journal literature.

Nevertheless, they provide useful computational comparisons.

## Serial Position Effects of Large Language Models (2024)

Research into serial-position effects investigates whether the location and ordering of information influence how language models process or retrieve it.

https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.15981

## Memory Effects in Large Language Models (2025)

Cao, Schooler and Zafarani investigate several memory phenomena in LLMs through paradigms derived from cognitive psychology.

Their reported experiments include effects associated with information load, repetition, overlapping information, false memory and generalisation.

The authors also report important differences between human and model behaviour.

This is particularly useful because a cross-field retained-state framework should search for both **similarities and failures of similarity**.

https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.17138

These works are useful comparison material.

They should not currently be given the same evidential weight as established peer-reviewed experimental literature.

# 10. The cross-field pattern

Taken together, these research programmes do **not** demonstrate one universal mechanism.

They demonstrate something narrower and more defensible.

Across several independently developed fields, researchers have found systems in which previous information can measurably affect subsequent processing or selection.

The terminology differs:

| Field | Example terminology |
|---|---|
| Decision neuroscience | Choice-history bias |
| Cognitive modelling | Evidence accumulation / drift bias |
| Visual perception | Serial dependence |
| Cognitive science | Continuity fields |
| Reinforcement learning | Primacy bias |
| Large language models | Serial-position and memory effects |
| Verrell's Law | Retained-state selection |

These terms are not interchangeable.

The mechanisms are not assumed to be identical.

The important convergence is the **research question**:

> When present conditions are controlled, what information from the previous trajectory remains capable of influencing what happens next?

---

# 11. Where Verrell's Law differs

Verrell's Law is not simply the statement:

> "history matters."

That proposition is already well established in many specialised domains.

The stronger research problem is whether history-sensitive phenomena can be described using a more general experimental structure based upon:

**retention → weighting → selection → observation → updated retention**

and whether the contribution of retained history can be isolated from the contribution of present conditions.

The framework therefore requires comparison between at least two explanatory models:

### Present-state model

Subsequent selection is adequately predicted from the present state alone.

### Retained-state model

Prediction improves when retained historical state is included.

The retained-state hypothesis earns support only when the second model produces a reproducible advantage under appropriate controls.

Most importantly, deliberate intervention on retained state should produce the predicted change.

Without intervention or ablation, correlation with history is not sufficient to establish causal influence.

---

# 12. What this literature does — and does not — establish

## It does establish

Independent research has demonstrated measurable forms of history dependence in:

- perceptual decisions;
- evidence accumulation;
- visual perception;
- sequential cognition;
- reinforcement learning;
- and increasingly, artificial language systems.

It therefore provides a substantial scientific basis for investigating retained-state influence as a serious experimental problem.

## It does not establish

These papers do **not** demonstrate that:

- all history-dependent phenomena share one mechanism;
- Verrell's Law is a fundamental law of physics;
- retained information necessarily acts non-locally;
- quantum mechanics is required to explain these results;
- consciousness is required;
- every apparent historical correlation represents causal retained-state influence.

Those remain separate questions.

---

# 13. The falsifiable question

The useful next step is therefore not to declare convergence to be proof.

It is to test the proposition more aggressively.

Take systems with controlled present conditions.

Construct distinguishable histories.

Restore or equalise the present observable state as far as experimentally possible.

Then ask:

> **Does historical divergence continue to predict subsequent selection?**

If it does, manipulate the retained historical variable.

If the predicted selection effect follows the intervention, the retained-state explanation becomes stronger.

If equivalent present conditions and controlled retained-state interventions produce no reproducible selection difference, the hypothesis loses support.

That ability to fail is essential.

---

# 14. Conclusion

Neuroscience, psychophysics, cognitive modelling and machine learning have arrived independently at an important observation:

**systems often carry their past into their next decision.**

The details differ.

Sometimes history attracts.

Sometimes it repels.

Sometimes early information becomes disproportionately influential.

Sometimes later evidence overrides it.

Sometimes the effect decays with time or intervening events.

Those differences are not obstacles to retained-state research.

They are precisely what needs to be measured.

Verrell's Law proposes a framework for asking whether these apparently separate phenomena expose a deeper and experimentally characterisable principle of retained-state selection.

The existing literature does not answer that question.

It gives us good reason to ask it.

## Research status

This document is a comparative research note within the **Verrell's Law Research Archive**.

The cited literature is evidence for specific forms of history-dependent behaviour within the systems studied by the respective authors.

It is **not presented as experimental validation of Verrell's Law itself**.

The purpose of this note is to identify convergent research questions, define the distinctions between them, and establish testable points of contact for future investigation.
