# Initial-State Weighting and Persistent Behavioural Bias

**Working Note — Verrell’s Law Research**  
**Author designation: VMR Core**  
**Organisation: Inappropriate Media Limited**  
**Status: Developing conceptual framework — open to testing, revision and falsification**

---

## Abstract

Human behaviour does not begin from a neutral state.

Before a person develops autobiographical memory, mature language, deliberate habits or a reflective identity, the nervous system already carries biological differences affecting attention, sensitivity, activity, inhibition, reward processing, emotional reactivity and response to stimuli.

This working note describes those early developmental differences as **initial-state weighting**.

The proposal is not that personality, behaviour or future life outcomes are fully predetermined before birth. It is the narrower claim that later experience enters a system that already possesses uneven sensitivities, thresholds and behavioural tendencies.

Experience therefore does not write onto a blank biological surface.

It modifies, reinforces, suppresses, redirects and reorganises a system whose starting conditions were already weighted.

---

## 1. Central proposition

The developing brain begins with inherited and developmentally produced differences that influence:

- which signals receive attention;
- how strongly different stimuli are registered;
- how readily reward and novelty attract behaviour;
- how easily impulses are inhibited;
- how strongly stress is experienced;
- how quickly attention shifts;
- which experiences become salient;
- and which response patterns are more likely to persist.

These differences are not conscious memories.

They are better understood as **prior organisational conditions** affecting how later information will be received, interpreted, retained and acted upon.

In Verrell’s Law terms:

> A developing informational system does not begin without history. Its earliest observable state already reflects inherited structure, prenatal development and prior biological organisation.

The system therefore contains meaningful weighting before it possesses language, autobiography or reflective self-awareness.

---

## 2. Initial-state weighting is not determinism

Initial-state weighting must not be confused with strict biological determinism.

A person is not born with every future decision, belief, habit or social behaviour already fixed.

Later behaviour emerges through interaction between:

1. inherited biological structure;
2. prenatal development;
3. early sensory and relational experience;
4. learning and reinforcement;
5. culture and environment;
6. injury, illness and physiological change;
7. conscious decisions;
8. repeated behaviour;
9. accumulated memory;
10. and changing social conditions.

The initial state constrains and biases development without uniquely dictating every outcome.

A person could live several substantially different lives while continuing to display recognisable underlying traits.

The expression may change significantly while the underlying weighting remains persistent.

---

## 3. ADHD as a worked example

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder provides a useful example of persistent developmental weighting.

ADHD is classified as a neurodevelopmental condition rather than a behaviour acquired only in adulthood. Research consistently indicates a substantial inherited contribution, although ADHD is complex, polygenic and not determined by any single gene.

Population-based heritability estimates are commonly reported in the broad range of approximately 70–80%, with some twin studies producing higher estimates.

Heritability is a statistical measure describing variation across a population.

It does **not** mean that a fixed percentage of one person’s behaviour is genetically predetermined.

The relevant point is that ADHD-related differences are strongly associated with developmental architecture affecting areas such as:

- attentional regulation;
- response inhibition;
- activity level;
- working-memory demand;
- reward sensitivity;
- novelty seeking;
- temporal organisation;
- task persistence;
- and the competition between immediate and delayed outcomes.

An infant does not display ADHD in the same visible form as an adult because the behavioural demands are different.

However, the developmental conditions from which later symptoms arise may already be present.

Life then shapes how those conditions are expressed.

The same underlying weighting may appear as disruptive impulsivity in one environment and as rapid association, risk-taking, creative exploration or intense subject-specific focus in another.

The architecture may remain persistent while its behavioural output changes with context.

---

## 4. Temperament before autobiography

Meaningful individual differences appear before mature personality and autobiographical memory.

Research into infant and childhood temperament shows early differences in:

- reactivity;
- sensitivity to stimulation;
- activity level;
- emotional intensity;
- recovery after distress;
- approach and withdrawal;
- adaptability;
- and attentional persistence.

These differences are partly influenced by genetics and development, while environmental conditions continue interacting with them.

A newborn does not need to consciously remember an event for its nervous system to already possess different thresholds and response tendencies.

These are meaningful biological differences already present in the developing nervous system.

They influence how later experience is registered, reinforced and interpreted.

---

## 5. Explicit memory and retained organisation

This model depends on a distinction between **explicit memory** and **retained organisation**.

### Explicit memory

Explicit memory includes retrievable content such as:

- an event;
- a face;
- a place;
- a sentence;
- a sequence;
- or a consciously recalled experience.

### Retained organisation

Retained organisation is broader.

It includes persistent physical and biological conditions produced by earlier developmental or experiential events, even where the original event cannot be consciously retrieved.

Examples include:

- neural connectivity;
- receptor sensitivity;
- hormonal regulation;
- learned response thresholds;
- conditioned tendencies;
- sensory sensitivity;
- stable attentional bias;
- and altered stress response.

A system can therefore carry the **consequence** of prior information without carrying a conscious **transcript** of it.

The past remains active through altered present structure.

---

## 6. A layered model of behavioural weighting

The proposed Verrell’s Law interpretation is:

> Every behavioural selection occurs from a presently weighted state, and that state contains retained consequences of earlier organisation.

At birth, some weighting derives from inheritance and prenatal development.

After birth, further weighting is added through perception, reinforcement, bodily state, social interaction, memory and repeated response.

The present state is therefore never informationally neutral.

It is the current expression of accumulated structural and experiential history.

This suggests five interacting layers.

### Layer 1 — Inherited weighting

Genetic and biological differences affecting the initial construction and regulation of the nervous system.

### Layer 2 — Prenatal developmental weighting

Conditions arising during embryonic and fetal development, including gene expression, cellular growth and the prenatal environment.

### Layer 3 — Early experiential weighting

Sensory, emotional and relational information acquired before mature language or autobiographical memory.

### Layer 4 — Learned behavioural weighting

Reinforcement, habit, conditioning, repeated response and social learning.

### Layer 5 — Reflective weighting

Conscious interpretation, deliberate decision-making, identity construction and self-directed behavioural change.

Later layers do not simply erase earlier ones.

They operate through them, interact with them and can modify their expression.

---

## 7. Persistent architecture and behavioural flexibility

A persistent trait should not be described as absolutely fixed or “set in stone.”

Brains remain plastic.

Behaviour can change through:

- development;
- treatment;
- medication;
- repeated practice;
- environment;
- social structure;
- stress;
- ageing;
- education;
- and deliberate adaptation.

However, plasticity does not mean that all starting architectures are equivalent or infinitely interchangeable.

A more accurate formulation is:

> Development preserves both continuity and change.

A person may learn to regulate impulsivity without becoming neurologically identical to someone who never experienced the same degree of impulsivity.

A person may build effective routines around attentional instability without eliminating the underlying attentional weighting.

Observed behaviour may therefore change more substantially than the architecture producing it.

---

## 8. Relationship to consciousness

This note does not claim that a completed conscious personality exists at conception or during early embryonic development.

It proposes something narrower:

> The biological system from which reflective consciousness later develops is already being differentially organised before reflective consciousness appears.

Consciousness therefore emerges from a system that already contains:

- asymmetry;
- developmental constraint;
- inherited variation;
- sensitivity differences;
- and biological history.

When conscious experience develops, it does not enter a blank processor.

It develops within an organism whose methods of receiving, prioritising and responding to information have already been partly shaped.

This may help explain why individuals differ deeply even before extensive deliberate self-construction has occurred.

---

## 9. Initial-state weighting and later memory

Initial-state weighting may also influence which later experiences become memorable.

A nervous system that is unusually sensitive to novelty, threat, reward, movement or social rejection may not encode all experiences equally.

The starting architecture can affect:

- what receives attention;
- what produces emotional intensity;
- what becomes reinforced;
- what is repeatedly recalled;
- what is avoided;
- and what becomes behaviourally dominant.

This creates a recursive process:

1. Initial weighting affects attention.
2. Attention affects which experiences are strongly encoded.
3. Strongly encoded experiences affect later expectation and behaviour.
4. Repeated behaviour alters the present state.
5. The updated state affects future selection.

The system therefore does not merely accumulate memory.

Its initial structure helps determine which information becomes memory in the first place.

## 10. Computational interpretation

Stable behavioural tendencies may be represented computationally as **weighted initial conditions** rather than as stored narrative memories.

A system does not need a transcript saying:

> “This agent has always been impulsive.”

Instead, impulsivity could be represented through persistent weighting applied to:

- reward timing;
- action thresholds;
- novelty value;
- inhibition cost;
- risk preference;
- and response selection.

This distinction matters.

A trait is not necessarily a remembered event.

It may be a persistent configuration affecting how events are selected and interpreted.

This provides a direct conceptual bridge to Collapse Aware AI.

The engineering question becomes:

> Can persistent behavioural tendencies be modelled as governed starting weights that continue influencing later selection without being treated as episodic memory?

That is consistent with the broader Verrell’s Law proposition that retained state affects future outcome.

It does not claim that current CAAI implementation reproduces human neurodevelopment.

It identifies a shared structural principle.

## 11. A formal predictive framing

For a defined behavioural system, let:

- \(I\) represent inherited or initial-state variables;
- \(H\) represent developmental and experiential history;
- \(X\) represent the present stimulus or environment;
- \(Y\) represent a later observable behaviour.

Initial-state weighting is scientifically relevant when:

\[
P(Y \mid X, H, I) \neq P(Y \mid X, H)
\]

That is, knowledge of the initial-state variables improves prediction of later behaviour beyond present stimulus and recorded history alone.

This does not prove immutability.

It shows that initial conditions retain explanatory or predictive value.

The strength of the effect can be measured by comparing:

- models using present conditions only;
- models using present conditions and life history;
- and models that also include stable developmental or biological weighting.

## 12. Testable implications

The framework must remain tied to evidence.

Relevant research questions include:

1. Which attentional, emotional and behavioural differences can be reliably detected before autobiographical memory develops?

2. Which early differences remain statistically stable into later childhood or adulthood?

3. How strongly do inherited traits affect which environmental experiences become reinforced?

4. Can persistent behavioural tendencies be modified without eliminating their underlying neurodevelopmental signatures?

5. Is later behaviour predicted more accurately by combining temperament, developmental history and present environment than by present circumstances alone?

6. Does early neurological weighting influence which memories become salient and repeatedly retrieved?

7. Can stable developmental biases be represented computationally as weighted initial conditions rather than narrative memories?

8. Can the influence of initial weighting be separated experimentally from later reinforcement?

9. Do individuals with different developmental profiles respond differently to identical immediate stimuli after present conditions are controlled?

10. Can early weighting be quantified without reducing a person to a fixed biological destiny?

These questions are open to measurement, disagreement and revision.

---

## 13. What would count as evidence?

Evidence supporting the framework could include:

- reproducible early differences in attention, reactivity or inhibition;
- longitudinal stability in selected behavioural traits;
- predictive improvement from including developmental variables;
- measurable interaction between inherited disposition and environment;
- persistent neurodevelopmental signatures despite behavioural adaptation;
- differential memory salience linked to initial attentional weighting;
- and computational models that predict behaviour more accurately when stable initial biases are included.

A strong result would not merely show that people differ.

It would show that identifiable early conditions retain measurable influence over later selection.

---

## 14. Falsification and failure conditions

The framework would be weakened if:

- early behavioural differences showed no meaningful persistence;
- initial-state variables added no predictive value after environmental and experiential factors were measured;
- all apparent weighting effects disappeared under longitudinal study;
- the model could not distinguish stable developmental bias from temporary state;
- or the term initial-state weighting added no measurable content beyond existing concepts such as temperament or neurodevelopment.

The concept must not be protected by redefining every later behaviour as evidence of initial weighting.

A valid test must state:

- which initial variable is being measured;
- when it is measured;
- which later outcome is predicted;
- which environmental factors are controlled;
- and what result would count against the proposal.

---

## 15. Current boundary of the claim

This document does **not** claim that:

- every personality trait is fixed before birth;
- genes determine every later behaviour;
- environment is secondary or irrelevant;
- ADHD has one universal mechanism;
- temperament and consciousness are identical;
- prenatal organisation is autobiographical memory;
- behavioural change is impossible;
- or a person’s future life is prewritten.

The present claim is narrower:

> Human development begins from a biologically and informationally weighted state. Later experience modifies that state but does not begin from neutrality.

The developing research proposition is:

> Early biological organisation retains measurable influence over later attention, memory, response selection and behavioural probability.

That proposition is consistent with existing work in neurodevelopment, temperament, behavioural genetics and gene–environment interaction, but remains a developing interpretation within Verrell’s Law rather than an established scientific law.

---

## Working formulation

> **A developing mind does not begin as an empty memory system. It begins as a weighted biological architecture. Experience supplies new information, but the meaning, priority and behavioural force of that information are partly determined by the structure already present.**

A stricter experimental version is:

> **Initial-state weighting is present when measurable early biological or developmental conditions improve prediction of later behaviour beyond present circumstances and recorded experience alone.**
