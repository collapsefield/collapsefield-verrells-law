# Initial-State Weighting and Persistent Behavioural Bias

**Working Note — Verrell’s Law Research**  
**Author: Verrell (M.R.)**  
**Inappropriate Media Limited**  
**Status: Preliminary conceptual model — open to testing and revision**

---

## Abstract

Human behaviour does not appear to begin from a neutral or uniformly configurable state.

Before a person accumulates autobiographical memory, social experience or deliberate habits, the developing nervous system already contains biological differences that affect attention, sensitivity, activity, inhibition, reward processing and response to stimuli.

This working note proposes that these early developmental differences may be described as **initial-state weighting**.

Initial-state weighting does not mean that a person’s future personality or behaviour is completely predetermined. It means that later information enters a system that already possesses uneven sensitivities, thresholds and behavioural tendencies.

Experience therefore does not construct behaviour on a blank surface. It modifies, reinforces, suppresses or redirects an already weighted developmental system.

---

## 1. The central proposition

The developing brain begins life with inherited and developmentally produced biases that influence:

- which signals receive attention;
- how strongly different stimuli are registered;
- which events become behaviourally important;
- how reward and novelty are valued;
- how readily impulses are inhibited;
- how strongly particular responses are reinforced;
- and which behavioural patterns remain persistent across time.

These biases are not necessarily conscious memories.

They are better understood as **prior organisational conditions** affecting how later information will be processed.

In Verrell’s Law terminology:

> A new informational system does not begin without history. Its first observable state already reflects inherited structure, developmental conditions and prior biological organisation.

The system may therefore possess meaningful informational weighting before it possesses language, autobiography or explicit self-awareness.

---

## 2. Behaviour is not completely written in advance

Initial-state weighting must not be confused with strict biological determinism.

A person is not born with every future decision, belief, habit or social behaviour already encoded.

Later behaviour emerges through interaction between:

1. inherited biological structure;
2. prenatal development;
3. early sensory and social experience;
4. learning and reinforcement;
5. culture and environment;
6. injury, illness and physiological change;
7. conscious decisions and repeated actions;
8. accumulated memory.

The initial system constrains and biases later development, but it does not uniquely dictate every outcome.

A person could live several substantially different lives while continuing to display recognisable underlying traits.

The expression may change while the underlying weighting remains persistent.

---

## 3. ADHD as a useful example

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder provides a strong example of a persistent developmental weighting pattern.

ADHD is classified as a neurodevelopmental disorder rather than a behaviour that an adult simply learns later in life. Genetic research consistently shows a substantial inherited contribution, although ADHD is complex and no single gene determines it. One major review estimated its heritability at approximately 74%, while more recent research reports twin-based estimates around 77–88%. :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0}

Heritability is a population-level statistical measure. It does not mean that a fixed percentage of one individual’s behaviour is genetically predetermined.

The relevant point is that ADHD-related differences are strongly associated with developmental architecture.

These differences may affect:

- attentional regulation;
- response inhibition;
- reward sensitivity;
- novelty seeking;
- activity level;
- working-memory demands;
- temporal organisation;
- and the competition between immediate and delayed outcomes.

An infant will not display ADHD in the same visible form as an adult because the tasks and behavioural demands are different. However, the developmental conditions from which later symptoms arise may already be present.

Life then determines how that architecture is expressed.

One person may display disruptive impulsivity. Another may channel similar weighting into rapid association, creative exploration, risk-taking or intense subject-specific focus.

The architecture is persistent, but its behavioural output is context-dependent.

## 4. Temperament before autobiography

Research into infant and childhood temperament supports the broader idea that meaningful individual differences appear before mature personality and autobiographical memory.

Twin and adoption studies indicate that early temperament is partly genetically influenced. Developmental research nevertheless shows that genetic tendencies and environmental conditions interact continuously rather than operating as isolated forces. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

This distinction matters.

A newborn does not need to remember a previous event consciously for its nervous system to possess:

- different response thresholds;
- different levels of reactivity;
- different sensitivity to stimulation;
- different recovery rates following distress;
- or different tendencies toward approach and withdrawal.

These are already informational differences within the operating system.

They influence what later experiences will mean to that system.

---

## 5. The distinction between memory and retained organisation

This model requires a wider distinction between **explicit memory** and **retained organisation**.

Explicit memory includes retrievable content such as:

- an event;
- a face;
- a location;
- a sentence;
- or a consciously recalled experience.

Retained organisation is broader.

It includes persistent structural conditions produced by earlier biological and developmental events, even where the system cannot retrieve those events as conscious memories.

Examples may include:

- neural connectivity;
- receptor sensitivity;
- hormonal regulation;
- learned response thresholds;
- conditioned tendencies;
- and stable attentional biases.

In this sense, a system can carry the consequence of prior information without carrying a conscious transcript of that information.

The past remains active through altered present structure.

---

## 6. Proposed Verrell’s Law interpretation

The proposed interpretation is:

> Every behavioural selection occurs from a presently weighted state, and that state contains retained consequences of earlier organisation.

At birth, some of that prior organisation derives from inheritance and prenatal development.

After birth, additional weighting is continually added through:

- perception;
- reinforcement;
- memory;
- bodily state;
- social interaction;
- and repeated behavioural selection.

The present state is therefore never informationally neutral.

It is the current expression of accumulated structural and experiential history.

This suggests a layered model:

### Layer 1 — Inherited weighting

Genetic and biological differences affecting the initial construction and regulation of the nervous system.

### Layer 2 — Prenatal developmental weighting

Conditions arising during embryonic and fetal development, including gene expression, cellular development and the prenatal environment.

### Layer 3 — Early experiential weighting

Sensory, emotional and relational information acquired before mature language or autobiographical memory.

### Layer 4 — Learned behavioural weighting

Reinforcement, habits, social conditioning and repeated response patterns.

### Layer 5 — Reflective weighting

Conscious interpretation, deliberate decision-making, identity construction and self-directed behavioural modification.

Later layers do not erase the earlier layers. They operate through them and can modify their expression.

---

## 7. Behavioural flexibility and persistent architecture

A persistent trait should not be described as absolutely “set in stone.”

Brains remain plastic. Behaviour changes with development, environment, treatment, repeated practice, stress, ageing and experience.

However, plasticity does not imply that all starting architectures are equivalent or infinitely interchangeable.

A more accurate formulation is:

> Development preserves both continuity and change.

The system can learn new strategies while retaining its underlying sensitivities.

Someone may learn to regulate impulsivity without becoming neurologically identical to a person who never experienced that degree of impulsivity.

Someone may construct effective routines around attentional instability without eliminating the underlying attentional weighting.

Observed behaviour may therefore change more substantially than the architecture producing it.

---

## 8. Relationship to consciousness

This note does not claim that a completed conscious personality exists at conception or during early embryonic development.

It proposes something narrower:

> The biological system from which consciousness later develops is already being differentially organised before reflective consciousness appears.

Consciousness therefore emerges from a system that already contains asymmetry, constraint and developmental history.

When conscious experience begins, it does not enter a blank processor.

It enters an organism whose methods of receiving, prioritising and responding to information have already been partly shaped.

This may help explain why conscious individuals differ so deeply even before extensive deliberate self-construction occurs.

---

## 9. Testable implications

This working model should remain tied to evidence.

Possible research questions include:

1. Which attentional, emotional and behavioural differences can be reliably detected before autobiographical memory develops?

2. Which early differences remain statistically stable into later childhood or adulthood?

3. How do inherited traits affect which environmental experiences become strongly reinforced?

4. Can persistent behavioural tendencies be modified without eliminating their underlying neurodevelopmental signatures?

5. Can later behaviour be predicted more accurately by combining initial temperament, developmental history and current environmental conditions than by using current circumstances alone?

6. Does early neurological weighting influence not only behaviour, but which memories become salient and repeatedly retrieved?

7. Can stable developmental biases be represented computationally as weighted initial conditions rather than as stored narrative memories?

The proposal should stand or fall according to observation, measurement and reproducibility.

---

## 10. Current boundary of the claim

This document does **not** claim:

- that every personality trait is fixed before birth;
- that genes determine every future behaviour;
- that environment is secondary or irrelevant;
- that ADHD has one universal biological mechanism;
- that temperament and consciousness are identical;
- or that prenatal organisation constitutes autobiographical memory.

The present claim is narrower and more defensible:

> Human development begins from a biologically and informationally weighted state. Later experience modifies that state, but does not begin from neutrality.

This is consistent with current evidence concerning neurodevelopment, temperament, genetic influence and gene–environment interaction. It remains a working interpretation within the wider Verrell’s Law framework rather than an established scientific law.

---

## Working formulation

> **A developing mind does not begin as an empty memory system. It begins as a weighted biological architecture. Experience supplies new information, but the meaning, priority and behavioural force of that information are partly determined by the structure already present.**
