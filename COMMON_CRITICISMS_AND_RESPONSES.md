# Common Criticisms and Current Responses

**Status:** Living clarification document

---

## Criticism: Is Verrell's Law morphic resonance?

Response:

No.

Verrell's Law does not depend on Rupert Sheldrake's morphic resonance hypothesis and should not be treated as an extension of it.

The framework should be evaluated on its own claims.

---

## Criticism: Has Verrell's Law been scientifically proven?

Response:

No.

Verrell's Law is currently a structured, testable hypothesis under active investigation.

Some engineering concepts have practical implementations.

Broader physical claims require independent validation.

---

## Criticism: Where is the memory stored?

Response:

Biological memory is known to involve physical neural processes.

Questions regarding broader informational persistence remain open research questions.

Mechanism unknown.

---

## Criticism: Is bias being treated as a mystical force?

Response:

No.

Within this project, bias means directional weighting.

Examples include:

- neural-network weights
- Bayesian priors
- preference structures
- memory influence
- selection pressure

---

## Criticism: Is Collapse Aware AI proof of Verrell's Law?

Response:

No.

Collapse Aware AI is an engineering implementation of memory-weighted behavioural selection.

A successful implementation would demonstrate practical utility, not automatic proof of a broader physical theory.

---

## Criticism: Is Verrell's Law replacing established physics?

Response:

No.

The framework does not claim to replace established physics.

Any broader physical interpretation must survive independent testing and falsification.

---

## Criticism: What would falsify the theory?

Response:

A domain-specific claim fails if retained history produces no measurable effect beyond ordinary present-state explanations under controlled conditions.

If history demonstrably does not matter, the claim fails in that domain.

---

## Criticism: Why focus on memory and information?

Response:

Because retained information is one of the most common mechanisms through which systems become path-dependent.

Brains, software, organisations, and learning systems all demonstrate history-conditioned behaviour.

The research question is how far that principle extends.
